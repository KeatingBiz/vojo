# Prosody, fillers, articulation, and whether a different stack grades better for less

Research date: 8 November 2026. Scope is scientific and product quality of Vojo’s post-transcript prosody (Praat autocorrelation, speaker-median semitones, monotone and uptalk thresholds, RMS energy, lexicon-plus-LLM fillers, no formants or pronunciation score) versus stacks a small team could adopt. Facts below are sourced. Inferences and gaps are separated. No code was changed.

Vojo’s published settings match Praat’s raw-autocorrelation defaults more than the 2023 filtered method: floor 75 Hz, time step 10 ms, voicing path costs as in Boersma’s tracker. Praat’s current standard ceiling for raw autocorrelation is 600 Hz; Vojo uses 500 Hz. ([Praat: Sound: To Pitch (ac)](https://praat.org/manual/Sound__To_Pitch__ac____.html); [Praat: how to choose a pitch analysis method](https://praat.org/manual/how_to_choose_a_pitch_analysis_method.html))

## How good is Praat autocorrelation F0 on short phone-mic monologues?

### Takeaway
Praat’s raw autocorrelation plus Viterbi path is still a serious F0 estimator for modal speech, and it is built to resist noise better than older cepstrum or comb methods, but octave errors, creak below the 75 Hz floor, and formant-harmonic confusions are documented failure modes. Nothing retrieved here measures those errors on 60-second smartphone monologues, so the 1.5 / 2.5 semitone monotone gates and the 4 semitones/second uptalk slope are unvalidated product thresholds sitting on top of a tracker that can both miss real pitch jumps and invent octave jumps.

### Cited Findings
- Praat’s pitch is the lag of the maximum of a window-normalized autocorrelation. At each frame (the manual’s example is every 10 ms) it keeps on the order of 15 peaks as candidates and tracks a path through them. Standard raw-autocorrelation costs: silence threshold 0.03, voicing threshold 0.45, octave cost 0.01 per octave, octave-jump cost 0.35, voiced/unvoiced cost 0.14. Octave cost exists because undertones of F0 are equally strong candidates even in a perfectly periodic signal. — [Praat manual, pitch analysis by raw autocorrelation](https://praat.org/manual/pitch_analysis_by_raw_autocorrelation.html)
- Boersma (1993) reports that local octave errors (detecting 2F as the best candidate) reach about 10% when amplitude modulation depth is 5% and F is near the pitch floor. The global path finder is described as able to remove those isolated local errors. The same paper says harmonics-to-noise ratio measurement wants at least about 6 periods per window, and that autocorrelation-domain HNR avoids the sidelobe problems of frequency-domain HNR. — [Boersma 1993, IFA Proceedings](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/archive/1993/1993-ifaproc17-Boersma.pdf)
- Since 2023 Praat also offers filtered autocorrelation: a Gaussian low-pass before the autocorrelation. Praat’s own manual says filtering reduces unwanted octave drops when the floor is 75 Hz, F0 is 200 Hz, and background noise makes the 10 ms lag look stronger than the true 5 ms lag. It also reduces unwanted octave rises when F1 is an integer multiple of F0 (example: F0 150 Hz, F1 450 Hz, ceiling 600 Hz), because the octave cost otherwise favors the higher candidate. Filtering also keeps more consonant–vowel transitions voiced, because raw autocorrelation treats fast formant movement as a loss of self-similarity. Filtered autocorrelation’s standard octave cost is 0.055 per octave, versus 0.01 for raw. — [Praat: how to choose a pitch analysis method](https://praat.org/manual/how_to_choose_a_pitch_analysis_method.html); [filtered autocorrelation parameters](https://praat.org/manual/pitch_analysis_by_filtered_autocorrelation.html)
- Creaky voice is low, often irregular F0 with a constricted glottis. Irregularity can make a tracker fail to report any F0. Period-doubling (multiple pulsing) produces an ambiguous period, so an octave jump can be a real change in vocal-fold vibration rather than an algorithm mistake. Praat’s manual says creak can go to about 30 Hz, below both the 50 Hz and 75 Hz floors people commonly set. — [Watkins et al., Interspeech 2024](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/watkins24_interspeech.pdf); [Praat Intro 4.2](https://praat.org/manual/Intro_4_2__Configuring_the_pitch_contour.html)
- De Cheveigné and Kawahara (2001): for voiced speech, F0 is the rate of vocal-fold vibration, but the vocal tract makes the waveform less periodic, and glottal fry, diplophonia, and irregular pulses often mean there is no single F0 to estimate. Their evaluation excluded those regions because a clean laryngograph reference cannot be defined there. They note that those regions are common, carry intonation, and that algorithms failing there is “natural,” while applications still need a graceful behavior. — [de Cheveigné & Kawahara 2001](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/deChevK01-ptrack.pdf)
- If F0 is wrong, H1–H2 and other harmonic voice-quality measures are taken at the wrong frequency. A University of Washington phonetics note treats irregular F0 as a reason to correct the track by hand, and distinguishes vocal fry (low but regularly periodic, modal constriction) as a subtype of creaky voice rather than a synonym. — [Yuan & Chai, UW Phonetics Lab, 31 May 2024](http://depts.washington.edu/phonlab/meeting_archive/05312024_Yuan_Chai.pdf)
- Ikuma et al. (ICVPB 2024 abstract) studied subharmonic errors on all 709 sustained /a/ tokens in the KayPENTAX Disordered Voice Database (16,174 frames of 50 ms). Praat was run with a 60 Hz floor (so its analysis window became 50 ms) after resampling to 8 kHz. CREPE (16 kHz, 64 ms window, 10 ms hop) and FCN-F0 were compared because an earlier study had found those models better than Praat on head-and-neck cancer voices. The abstract argues that a subharmonic of a nonmodal voice can be the true glottal rate, so periodicity alone cannot choose. The retrieved text does not include the numeric frame-accuracy results. — [Ikuma et al. abstract PDF](https://www.medschool.lsuhsc.edu/otorhinolaryngology/docs/Ikuma_ICVPB2024_Abstract_ML_Pitch.pdf)
- A small microphone-speech comparison (arxiv 2206.14357; 10 female and 10 male utterances, not a phone-noise corpus) found gross pitch errors rare and present only in CREPE. On their figure of merit, YAAPT beat pYIN by one rank point and pYIN beat CREPE by one. CREPE’s voicing errors on that set were about 7% and 19% depending on direction. The authors state CREPE’s training set was synthetic music and no real voice, and they explicitly say they did not test noise. — [Joglekar & Sepúlveda, arXiv:2206.14357](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.14357)

### Inferences
- Vojo’s floor of 75 Hz will drop or octave-mislabel a large part of vocal fry and many male creaky phrase endings. Those frames either disappear from the semitone standard deviation (monotone looks worse or better depending on which tails are cut) or enter as octave outliers (variety looks larger than it is). Median-normalization does not fix an octave error: an octave is 12 semitones, far above the 1.5 semitone monotone gate.
- A single global median F0 over a 60 s answer is a reasonable speaker norm for one monologue, and it matches the semitone scale eGeMAPS uses. It does not separate a speaker’s habitual range from one flat answer, and it is biased if unvoiced noise frames or octave errors enter the median.
- Phrase-final lengthening and continuation rises are a plausible source of uptalk false positives for a least-squares slope on the last 0.40 s of every transcript sentence, but that specific failure was not measured in the sources retrieved. Transcript sentence boundaries from `.?!` will also split or merge statements when the recognizer’s punctuation is wrong, so the uptalk unit is only as good as the STT punctuation.
- Raw RMS energy is not perceived loudness. It will move with mic distance, automatic gain, and breath noise. eGeMAPS treats auditory loudness, not RMS, as the intensity feature. See the alternatives section.
- Switching Vojo from raw to filtered autocorrelation is a configuration change inside the same Praat/Parselmouth stack, not a new model. Praat’s own 2023 write-up is the strongest sourced argument that this helps exactly the noise and formant octave errors a phone mic produces. It is not a published accuracy number on smartphone monologues.

### Gaps
- No retrieved study reports Praat raw-autocorrelation gross-error or octave-error rates on short smartphone monologues (office noise, Bluetooth, AGC, 16 kHz AAC).
- No retrieved perceptual study validates “monotone if F0 standard deviation < 1.5 semitones; full score at ≥ 2.5 semitones” or “uptalk if terminal slope > 4 semitones/second over 0.40 s.”
- No retrieved measurement of uptalk false positives caused by phrase-final lengthening, listing rises, or continuation rises (H-H% / L-H% boundary tones).
- Ikuma’s numeric comparison of CREPE versus Praat on disordered voice was truncated in the retrieved abstract.
- A GitHub pitch-benchmark README appeared in search snippets with Praat pitch-F1@50 cents of 0.651 at 525× realtime and CREPE at 0.689 and 0.4× realtime. Fetching the repository page did not return that table. Those figures are not used here.

## What coaches listen for versus what automatic scores can reliably measure

### Takeaway
Toastmasters, the best-documented public-speaking rubric retrieved, grades vocal variety as the joint use of tone, speed, and volume, and separately grades clarity of language, articulation, pausing, voice quality (nasal, breathy, harsh), and naturalness. Rate, pausing, filler counts, and a speaker-normalized F0 spread are the pieces with a direct acoustic definition. Resonance, fry-as-style, “open” versus “tight” voice, answer structure, and whether variety matched the content are coach judgments, not reliable meter readings.

### Cited Findings
- Toastmasters evaluation resource 8104E1 (file version dated in the CDN path around 2025) scores Clarity (“spoken language is clear and is easily understood”) separately from Vocal Variety (“uses tone, speed, and volume as tools”), plus eye contact, gestures, audience awareness, comfort, and interest. The top vocal-variety band is “uses the tools of tone, speed, and volume to perfection.” — [Toastmasters 8104E1 PDF](https://content.toastmasters.org/image/upload/v1741989017/8104E1-evaluation-resource-ff.pdf)
- The vocal-variety-only Speech Profile (8104E2) rates loudness (too quiet / too loud, flat / vibrant), pitch (too high / too low, successfully varied, shrill / full), voice quality (nasal–breathy–open, harsh/raspy–mellow, lifeless–engaging), articulation and word usage (mumbling, mispronunciations, poor versus effective pausing), timing or rate (jerky/smooth, slow/fast, unvaried/varied, hesitant/fluent), and overall vocal variety (emotionless versus exaggerated, strained versus natural). — [Toastmasters 8104E2 PDF](https://ccdn.toastmasters.org/medias/files/department-documents/education-documents/evaluation-resources/english/8104e2-speech-profile-ff.pdf)
- An older Toastmasters “Your Speaking Voice” profile uses the same dimensions and adds articulatory causes: slushy, lazy lips, tangled tongue, tight jaw, mumbling, mispronunciation. — [Toastmasters 199, Your Speaking Voice](https://ccdn.toastmasters.org/medias/files/department-documents/education-documents/199-your-speaking-voice.pdf)
- A 2023 club evaluation sheet (Kathy Shine, DTM) splits vocal variety into pitch range, volume (including whisper for drama), and tone as emotional content that a listener can stand for a long time. — [Assessment of the Audible Message, updated July 2023](https://professionalcareerspeakers.toastmastersclubs.org/file/129254/)
- Eyben et al. designed eGeMAPS for parameters that index affective physiological change and can be extracted automatically: log-F0, jitter, shimmer, HNR, formants, auditory loudness, spectral balance (alpha ratio, Hammarberg index, spectral slope), H1–H2, H1–A3, and pause/voicing timing. They argue F0 and intensity are the consistent cues to vocal arousal, and that formants and spectral slope carry timbre and voice-quality information. On acted and induced emotion corpora, the 88-parameter eGeMAPS set matched much larger brute-force sets for binary arousal (average UAR about 79.7 versus 78.0 for the large ComParE set) and was close on valence (66.4 versus 67.2). Those are emotion-classification numbers, not coaching-score agreement. — [Eyben et al. 2016, IEEE TAC](https://lab-msp.com/MSP/publications/Eyben_2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- Overlap with what Vojo already computes, if the tracker is clean: speaking rate (needs word times from STT, not Praat), pause structure (eGeMAPS approximates this with unvoiced-region duration; Vojo’s RMS does not), pitch variety (semitone standard deviation is a blunt version of “successfully varied” versus “flat”), and a weak loudness proxy.
- Not covered, and not reliably covered by F0 standard deviation: whether the variety was in the right places, shrill versus full, nasal versus open, breathy versus clear, harsh versus mellow, mumbling versus crisp consonants, and whether emotion was exaggerated. HNR, H1–H2, and formant bandwidths are the acoustic cousins of breathy/pressed/nasal, but they are frame measures with well-known tracker dependence, not a coach’s “voice quality” grade.
- Vocal fry is a Toastmasters-adjacent coaching topic (harsh/lifeless/creaky) and a known Praat failure, not a feature Vojo scores. A low F0 standard deviation can coexist with heavy fry.
- Filler habits are in coach practice and in every commercial app retrieved below. They are only partly acoustic. “Uh/um” are stable words; “like / so / you know” are ambiguous discourse markers. Classic NIST metadata systems treated that ambiguity with rules and parsers, not with an F0 detector (see alternatives).
- Answer structure (point, support, close) is in the Toastmasters “interest” and “well supported” bands and in Yoodli’s “conciseness and structure” claim. It is a transcript task. Grading it from acoustics would be the wrong tool.

### Gaps
- No retrieved inter-rater reliability for Toastmasters vocal-variety scores, and no study mapping those scores onto F0 standard deviation, RMS, or filler counts.
- No 2023–2026 public-speaking textbook or coach-certification rubric (beyond Toastmasters and one club sheet) was fetched. Pedagogy sources such as Lucas, Grant, or specific voice-coach methods were not retrieved.
- Resonance as a coaching term (chest/head, “placement”) has no clean short-time acoustic target in the sources retrieved. Formants are related and are not the same judgment.

## Open or cheap alternatives for the same signals

### Takeaway
For a 60-second clip, openSMILE/eGeMAPS and Praat are CPU-cheap and interpretable. The neural pitch model with the best sourced speech numbers is PENN’s FCNF0++, not stock CREPE: on clean read speech it is more accurate than pYIN and DIO and still faster than realtime on CPU. CREPE was trained on synthetic music, is slower, and does not win a small real-speech test. emotion2vec and wav2vec2/HuBERT embeddings improve emotion classification; they do not emit a monotone or uptalk score. Text disfluency taggers are accurate on careful Switchboard transcripts and degrade off-domain. None of these have a published win on noisy mobile monologues.

### Cited Findings
- eGeMAPS is 88 utterance-level numbers: 62 in the minimal GeMAPS set plus 26 from MFCCs 1–4, spectral flux, extra formant bandwidths, and equivalent sound level. Low-level features include log-F0 in semitones re 27.5 Hz, jitter, shimmer, F1–F3 frequency, F1 bandwidth, loudness, HNR, alpha ratio, Hammarberg index, spectral slopes, H1–H2, H1–A3, plus timing: loudness-peak rate, mean and standard deviation of voiced and unvoiced region length, and voiced regions per second as a pseudo-syllable rate. Implementation is openSMILE. v01b fixed a numerical-stability issue; current Python bindings also expose eGeMAPSv02. — [Eyben et al. 2016](https://lab-msp.com/MSP/publications/Eyben_2016.pdf); [openSMILE Python feature sets](https://audeering.github.io/opensmile-python/api/opensmile.FeatureSet.html); [audEERING on openSMILE 3.0](https://www.audeering.com/introducing-opensmile-3-0/)
- CREPE (Kim et al., 2018) is a 6-layer CNN on the waveform, 16 kHz, 64 ms frames, hop 10 ms, 360 pitch bins. On synthetic music (RWC-synth, MDB-stem-synth) raw pitch accuracy at 50 cents was 0.999 and 0.967, ahead of pYIN and SWIPE, and it held up better than pYIN under added white, pink, and pub noise. Brown noise was the exception (pYIN almost unaffected). The paper’s evaluation sets are music, not speech. The authors note the mobile-phone speech case as motivation and do not report it. — [Kim et al., CREPE](https://arxiv.org/html/1802.06182v1)
- Morrison et al. (PENN, arXiv:2301.12258) train pitch and periodicity networks on speech and music. PTDB is 4,718 English utterances, 9.6 hours, with laryngograph F0 and voicing labels. Pooled with MDB-stem-synth, FCNF0++ (local expected-value decoding) had pitch error 12.45 cents, raw pitch accuracy 0.9825, raw chroma accuracy 0.9853. PYIN was 110.5 cents and RPA 0.8477. DIO+Stonemask was RPA 0.6961. The gap between RPA and RCA is their indicator of octave errors. Real-time factor on an Intel i9-9820X, 10 ms hop, including audio load and save: FCNF0++ CPU 0.0892 (about 11× realtime), GPU (RTX 3090) 0.0024 (about 400× realtime). CREPE in the same table: RPA 0.9748, CPU RTF 0.3574, GPU RTF 0.0093. PYIN CPU RTF 0.0639. DIO CPU RTF 0.0177. They call FCNF0++ the useful default because accuracy matches the best net and it is about an order of magnitude faster than DeepF0++. — [Morrison et al., arXiv:2301.12258](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.12258)
- WORLD (Morise) exposes DIO and Harvest as its pitch estimators. PENN reports DIO as fast and, on PTDB plus synthetic music, much less accurate than FCNF0++ (RPA 0.696 versus 0.983). Harvest was not in that table. — [WORLD repository](https://github.com/mmorise/World); [Morrison et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.12258)
- emotion2vec (Ma et al., ACL 2024 Findings) is a self-supervised emotion representation pre-trained on 262 hours of unlabeled emotional speech. Frozen features plus a linear layer, SUPERB-style, on IEMOCAP: emotion2vec base about 93.8M parameters reaches weighted accuracy 71.79 to 74.48 depending on the fold protocol in Table 2. Same protocol: wav2vec 2.0 base 63.43, HuBERT base 64.92, WavLM base 65.94, WavLM+ 67.98. The task is categorical speech-emotion recognition, plus song emotion, conversation emotion, and sentiment. It does not output F0, filler tags, or an articulation score. Pre-training used 4 A10 GPUs; inference cost for a 60 s clip is not reported. — [Ma et al. 2024 PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.931.pdf)
- Switchboard disfluency work is mostly transcript repair detection, not an acoustic “um” detector. Zayats et al. report disfluency F-score 0.841 on Switchboard with stacked Max-Margin Markov networks, starting by detecting filler words in the transcript. — [Zayats, Ostendorf & Hajishirzi, NAACL 2013](https://aclanthology.org/N13-1102.pdf)
- Lease et al. (RT-04) detected filled pauses with rules: more than 95% of filled pauses were “uh, um, eh, ah,” and those words were filled pauses more than 95% of the time, so they labeled every occurrence as a filled pause. Discourse markers (“you know,” “i mean,” “like,” “so,” “well,” …) needed context and parse rules. Deterministic filler rules had about 19% error after POS and tree constraints, versus about 30% if every ambiguous word was always called a discourse marker. — [Lease, Johnson & Charniak, RT-04](https://mattlease.com/papers/rt04-paper.pdf)
- Off-domain, a Switchboard-trained disfluency model scored F1 39.7 on Supreme Court oral-argument transcripts; careful in-domain training scored 26.1; their adapted system reached 62.2. The drop is the relevant caution for a tagger trained on telephone conversations and applied to coached monologues. — [Zayats, Ostendorf & Hajishirzi, Unediting](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~hannaneh/papers/uneditor.pdf)
- A 2016 bidirectional LSTM is described as state of the art on Switchboard disfluency and correction detection, with a specific gain on non-repetition repairs. The retrieved landing page does not include the F1 table. — [Interspeech 2016-1247](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2016-1247)

### Inferences
- Cost for one 60 s clip, using only the reported real-time factors and ignoring cold start: FCNF0++ is on the order of 5 s of one i9 core, or a fraction of a second on a 3090 if the GPU is already warm. CREPE on that same CPU table is about 21 s. Praat, pYIN, and DIO are well under 10 s and usually well under 1 s. At a few cents of cloud CPU per hour, all of these are rounding error next to a speech-to-text call. The accuracy case for a GPU pitch model on 60 s clips is weak.
- eGeMAPS is the highest-value free add, not a replacement for an explicit monotone rule. It already contains speaker-comparable F0 functionals, loudness (better than RMS), HNR and H1–H2 (breathy versus modal, with the creak caveats above), formant summaries, and pause/voicing timing. It will not label “uptalk” unless someone computes a terminal slope themselves. Praat can do that slope more transparently.
- Stock CREPE is a poor swap for Praat on this product: music training, slower CPU, and a small speech test where it did not beat YAAPT or pYIN. PENN is the neural tracker that was actually built and scored for speech. Its headline numbers are on clean read speech with a laryngograph, so the gain on a phone monologue is an extrapolation.
- wav2vec2 / HuBERT / emotion2vec are feature extractors. Turning them into a coaching score requires labels Vojo does not get from Praat (human monotone, fry, clarity). Without that fine-tune they are an opaque embedding, and emotion2vec’s win is emotion class accuracy around the low 70s on acted IEMOCAP, which is not a delivery grade.
- Vojo’s lexicon-plus-LLM check for ambiguous fillers matches the structure of the best simple system in the NIST filler literature: deterministic “uh/um,” and a context model only for “like/so.” An acoustic disfluency detector is the wrong purchase if the transcript is already in hand. A Switchboard edit detector would add repetitions and restarts, which coaches do care about, and it will need in-domain transcripts or it is likely to sit near the 40 F1 off-domain result rather than the 0.84 in-domain result.
- No NVIDIA conversational-disfluency product was verified in this pass.

### Gaps
- No CPU timing for openSMILE eGeMAPSv02 on a 60 s, 16 kHz clip.
- No head-to-head of Praat filtered autocorrelation versus FCNF0++ on mobile or noisy speech.
- No sourced inference latency for emotion2vec or HuBERT-base on 60 s CPU.
- Harvest (WORLD) accuracy versus Praat was not in the PENN table.
- The 2016 BLSTM’s exact Switchboard F1 was not in the retrieved page.
- No 2023–2026 paper retrieved that evaluates these trackers on smartphone monologues.

## Is there a cheaper path that is also higher quality?

### Takeaway
Yes, but it is not “one audio language model grades everything.” Local Praat plus local eGeMAPS is both cheaper and more inspectable than a pronunciation API or an audio LLM, and it covers the coach-relevant signals that have a real acoustic definition. The quality upgrade inside that budget is filtered autocorrelation, auditory loudness instead of RMS, HNR or H1–H2 as a voice-quality flag, and pause timing. An audio LLM is cheap enough on Gemini’s published rates to write comments, and it is the wrong instrument for the numeric monotone and uptalk scores. Keep deterministic measures for anything a user might dispute.

### Cited Findings
- Gemini 2.5 Flash standard pricing, on the Google pricing page last updated 2026-10-30: audio input $1.00 per 1M tokens; text/image/video input $0.30 per 1M; output, including thinking tokens, $2.50 per 1M. ### Inferences
- Ranked by cost for a 60 s answer, ignoring the speech-to-text Vojo already pays: local Praat or eGeMAPS at essentially $0; Gemini live audio-in at about $0.005 plus text out; Azure pronunciation, if the $1 to $1.32 per hour figures hold, at about $0.02 plus an unknown prosody add-on; Speechace Premium at $0.05; ELSA unscripted at about $0.08. A retired GPT-4o audio model cannot be priced per minute from the official page. Local acoustics are the cheap end. The audio LLM is the next-cheapest published rate and still costs money on every answer; Praat does not.
- Quality is not ordered the same way. Praat’s F0, loudness, HNR, and pause timings are reproducible: the same wav yields the same number. An audio LLM’s “monotone: 62” can change with temperature, prompt, and model version, and the sources here contain no calibration study against F0 standard deviation or against coaches. Using Gemini to rewrite feedback from deterministic features is a different, safer design than asking it to hear the score.
- Latency: local Praat/eGeMAPS finishes in well under a second to a few seconds on CPU after the wav is in memory. A Gemini generate call adds a network round trip and whatever thinking tokens are enabled (billed as output). That is acceptable for an async daily grade and is not more reliable.
- The higher-quality cheap path that the sources actually support: keep the existing STT for words; switch Praat to filtered autocorrelation and keep the semitone-relative-to-median summary; replace RMS with eGeMAPS loudness; add HNR or H1–H2 and unvoiced-region duration as voice-quality and pausing features; keep the filler lexicon, with the LLM only on ambiguous discourse markers. Add PENN only if a labeled set of phone recordings shows Praat octave errors that filtered autocorrelation still misses. Do not add CREPE, emotion2vec, or a disfluency tagger as the first purchase.
- One audio model that “scores everything” is cheaper than Speechace or ELSA and more expensive than Praat. It can plausibly comment on answer structure, which acoustics cannot. It should not be the system of record for pitch variety, uptalk, or filler counts, because those already have deterministic estimators and the LLM error mode is an uncalibrated number.

## Can phoneme-level ASR or pronunciation APIs score clarity on spontaneous answers?

### Takeaway
Azure, Speechace, and ELSA all advertise an unscripted mode, so the “read-aloud only” limitation is false for those three. The score they return is still a language-learning accuracy score: how close phones are to a native-speaker target, plus fluency and, on some plans, prosody. That is a weak proxy for a fluent speaker’s mumbling or articulation clarity, and it will move with accent. Prices for a 60-second spontaneous answer are about $0.02 (Azure base, prosody extra, price-list digits unverified) to $0.05 (Speechace) to $0.08 (ELSA). Montreal Forced Aligner and a raw wav2vec2 phoneme recognizer were not shown in retrieved docs to emit a clarity score.

### Cited Findings
- Azure: `ReferenceText` is optional. Omit it for unscripted assessment (“speaking” scenario); set it for scripted assessment (“reading” scenario). Unscripted results include `AccuracyScore` at phoneme, syllable (en-US only), word, and full-text level, defined as how closely the phonemes match a native speaker’s pronunciation; `FluencyScore` (silent breaks between words versus a native-speaker pattern); and `ProsodyScore` (stress, intonation, speaking speed, rhythm). Prosody assessment is en-US only. Microsoft’s own note: the unscripted speech-to-text model differs from Azure’s normal speech-to-text, and if the recognized text needs to be accurate they recommend running normal speech-to-text first and then a scripted assessment. Mispronunciation is flagged when word accuracy is below 60. Monotone is an error type on the whole utterance, copied onto every word, and Microsoft says the syllable-pitch-delta confidence thresholds are there so the caller can override the built-in monotone decision. Suggested break-confidence threshold is 0.75. Phoneme names are IPA for en-US. — [Microsoft Learn, pronunciation assessment](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/how-to-pronunciation-assessment)
- The same docs: accuracy, fluency, completeness, and miscue are in the base speech-to-text price; prosody is not. Completeness requires a reference text, so it is scripted-only. — [Pronunciation assessment tool pricing table](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/pronunciation-assessment-tool)
- Speechace Premium is the plan that adds spontaneous open-ended activities: transcription, language detection, IELTS/CEFR/PTE/TOEIC speaking scores, vocabulary, grammar, coherence, relevance, and task achievement, max audio 2 minutes, $0.0125 per 15 seconds. Pro, which is not the spontaneous tier, adds fluency, intonation, pausing, pitch, speaking rate, articulation rate, and words correct per minute, max 45 seconds, $0.008 per 15 seconds. Languages listed: English (US, UK), French (FR, CA), Spanish (ES, MX). — [Speechace plans](https://www.speechace.com/api-plans/)
- ELSA documents two inputs: unscripted, where the server recognizes what was said and then scores it, and scripted, where the text is given. Outputs listed for the API include sentence, word, and phoneme scores; mispronunciation hints; word stress, reading speed, mispronunciation rate, and pausing; fluency and intonation; and a spoken IELTS estimate. Unscripted standard price on the page is $0.02 per 15 seconds. — [ELSA API](https://elsaspeak.com/en/elsa-api/)
- A 2026 comparison (TonePerfect, a vendor) says Speechmatics was not the pronunciation product in their table; Speechmatics’ own pricing page fetched here is speech-to-text, “from $0.121/hr,” with credits per hour of audio. No pronunciation-assessment SKU was visible in that fetch. — [Speechmatics pricing](https://www.speechmatics.com/pricing); [TonePerfect API comparison](https://api.toneperfect.app/best-pronunciation-assessment-api/)
- TonePerfect’s comparison, which is a competitor’s page and should be read as such, says Azure lists 33 pronunciation locales as of 10 September 2026 and accepts unscripted speech; Speechace and ELSA are the English-focused exam-style APIs; TonePerfect itself is scripted only. — [TonePerfect, 10 Sep 2026](https://api.toneperfect.app/compare/azure-pronunciation-assessment/)

### Inferences
- Unscripted support answers the product question “can we send a spontaneous answer with no script?” Yes for Azure, Speechace Premium, and ELSA. It does not answer “does the score mean the speaker was easy to understand?” Azure’s accuracy definition is distance from a native-speaker phone model. A clear Indian or Scottish English answer can score as inaccurate. A careful but mumbled native answer can score as accurate if the phones still match the model. Toastmasters articulation (mumbling, lazy articulators, tight jaw) is not that number.
- Microsoft’s warning that unscripted recognition is a different, less trusted transcript is material for Vojo: if phoneme errors are anchored to a bad transcript, the clarity score is partly an ASR error. Their recommended repair is “STT, then scripted assessment,” which doubles the audio billing and assumes the first transcript is the text the speaker intended. For a practice answer there is no intended script, so scripted mode grades agreement with the recognizer, not agreement with a target sentence.
- Speechace’s articulation rate and pausing live on the Pro (shorter, not the open-ended) tier. The spontaneous tier is an exam rubric (IELTS-style vocabulary, grammar, coherence). That is closer to answer quality than to consonant clarity, and it overlaps the LLM feedback Vojo can already run on the transcript.
- At $0.05 to $0.08 per answer, pronunciation APIs cost more than Gemini audio-in and infinitely more than Praat, for a construct (native-likeness) Vojo’s daily English answers mostly do not need. They are justified if the product becomes accent coaching. They are not the first buy for “this answer was mushy.”
- Forced alignment (Montreal Forced Aligner) and a wav2vec2 phoneme decoder were not documented in this pass as scoring services. Alignment needs a transcript and a dictionary; it produces times, not a clarity grade. Phoneme posterior entropy could be built into a clarity feature, and no retrieved paper validates that feature on spontaneous native monologues. Treat that as an open experiment, not a 2026 API.

### Gaps
- No independent correlation of Azure, Speechace, or ELSA unscripted scores with human ratings of articulation clarity on fluent, spontaneous, native-speaker answers (as opposed to L2 read-aloud or IELTS speaking).
- Azure prosody add-on price in dollars was not rendered on the official pricing page.
- Speechmatics pronunciation assessment: not found. Absence from one pricing fetch is not proof they have no such API.
- Montreal Forced Aligner documentation and wav2vec2-phoneme papers were not fetched in this pass.
- ELSA Premium unscripted price is $0.025 on a competitor’s table and only the $0.02 standard rate was clearly on ELSA’s own page in the snippets retrieved. Confirm on a fresh ELSA page before quoting $0.025.
