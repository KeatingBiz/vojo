# Custom articulation and speaking-coach model feasibility (November 2026)

Scope: whether a small Expo app team could train its own articulation and speaking-coach model, “incredibly accurate,” on real coaches, instead of Vojo’s hybrid (Deepgram or AssemblyAI transcript with fillers kept, local Praat for pitch and uptalk, deterministic filler/pace/prosody scores, Claude Haiku for content judgment and coaching sentences). Feasibility only. Today is 8 November 2026. Numbers below are from the cited source unless the Inferences section marks them as judgment.

## What would the training target be, and which of those targets can actually be learned from coach labels?

### Takeaway
Only some of the four targets are learnable from coach labels, and none of them supports a claim of incredible accuracy. Countable delivery traits do not need a learned model. Holistic or dimension ratings can be predicted only up to how much coaches agree. Coach-like prose can be imitated without being factually right. Phone-level articulation needs phone labels, not a vibe score.

### Cited Findings
- SpeechRater’s early design already treats fluency, vocabulary diversity, grammar, and pronunciation as features fed to a regression, not as something a single end-to-end model invents. On TOEFL Practice Online, machine–human correlation on six-item sets was r = 0.57 versus inter-human r = 0.74; exact agreement on single items was 57.8% versus inter-human 57.2%. — [Zechner et al. 2009, Speech Communication](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1016/j.specom.2009.04.009)
- A later SpeechRater description trains multiple linear regression on about 1,200 TOEFL Practice Online responses and evaluates on about 300 speakers (about 1,800 responses). Aggregated speaker-level machine–human r = 0.61 and quadratic weighted kappa = 0.53, against human inter-rater r = 0.63 and quadratic weighted kappa = 0.57 on the same data. The same source says most features cover delivery, few cover language use, and none (as of that write-up) cover topic development. Undergraduate listeners’ comprehension of the same responses correlated 0.97 and 0.81 with human scores on two forms, versus 0.55 and 0.37 with SpeechRater. — [Handbook of Test Development, reliability and validity chapter (secondary full text)](https://ebrary.net/76697/education/reliability_validity)
- Operational TOEFL iBT speaking uses one human rater plus one SpeechRater score per task. Assessment reliability (PRMSE) was 0.76 for SpeechRater only, 0.75 for human raters only, and 0.83 when both are combined. — [ETS TOEFL Research Insight Series, Volume 3](https://www.in.ets.org/content/dam/ets-india/pdfs/toefl/toefl-ibt-insight-s1v3.pdf)
- A 2025 three-level meta-analysis of automated speech evaluation (67 studies, 392 effect sizes) reports overall human–machine r = .654. By construct: delivery r = .784, overall proficiency r = .686, fluency r = .618, pronunciation r = .606, content r = .574, grammar and vocabulary r = .499. Correlations rose from traditional ML (r = .597) to deep learning (r = .641) to transformers (r = .680). — [Language Testing meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/02655322251387816)
- On spontaneous public-speaking audio only, five student raters scored 684 thirty-second clips from 228 ICASSP 2011 speakers and were told to ignore verbal content and pronunciation. Pairwise Spearman ρ = .470; Cronbach’s α = 0.840. Speaker-independent regression from acoustic features reached ρ = 0.60; three-way classification reached 59.5% unweighted average recall. — [Azaïs et al., Interspeech 2015](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/azais15_interspeech.pdf)
- An ETS prototype public-speaking rubric, scored by expert and nonexpert raters on 17 speakers and 4 tasks, had expert–nonexpert reliability from .23 to .71 by dimension. Lowest: word choice .23 and effectual persuasion .41. Higher: vocal expression .75, visual aids .65. Holistic .63. The paper says even expert oral-communication raters had trouble agreeing on persuasion and word choice, and that task, rubric, and training need heavy investment before large-scale use. — [ETS Research Report, public speaking human-scoring quality](https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12083)
- A multimodal public-speaking model (speech, text, and motion) on 14 speakers in leave-one-speaker-out cross-validation reached r = 0.765 (glmnet) and r = 0.750 (SVM) on prepared speeches, versus r = 0.625 and r = 0.571 from text plus speech alone. Impromptu speeches were lower: r = 0.592 and r = 0.525 multimodal. — [Chen et al., Journal of Learning Analytics 2016](https://doi.org/10.18608/jla.2016.32.13)
- A separate multimodal ensemble reported r = 0.745 with expert judges on overall public-speaking performance, and multimodal models beat single modalities. The abstract does not give the speaker count. — [ICMI 2015 multimodal public speaking assessment](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2818346.2820762)
- Phone-level pronunciation work uses a different label than a coach’s holistic score. SpeechOcean762 has 2,500 labeled training utterances and 2,500 evaluation utterances, each scored by 5 language experts. Microsoft reports Pearson correlation on that set of 0.6242 without an “OR” component and 0.6562 with it, against a human-parity figure of 0.6787 (average pairwise expert correlation). Goodness-of-pronunciation (GOPT) was 0.5950 on the same table. — [Microsoft speech-service blog on hierarchical transformer pronunciation assessment](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azure-ai-services-blog/speech-service-update-%E2%80%93-hierarchical-transformer-for-pronunciation-assessment/3740866)
- Expert annotators localizing pronunciation errors agreed only moderately: kappa 0.492 on error location for 12 responses (1,443 words), versus crowd kappa 0.297 on 143 responses. Correlation on the *count* of errors was r = 0.53 for experts and r = 0.71 for the crowd. — [Loukina et al., Interspeech 2015](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/loukina15b_interspeech.pdf)
- Generative models asked to score pronunciation do not match experts on the fine dimensions. Three experts on 180 samples had ICC .882 with each other after a standardization workshop. Against that average, a generative system had ICC .637 and r = .564 on fluency, but ICC .210 and r = .271 on individual phonemes. Stress, rhythm, intonation, linking, reduction, and clarity sat between poor and moderate ICC (.430–.512). The generative scores were systematically higher on every sub-element (paired t-test p < .001). — [PLOS One, human vs generative pronunciation scoring](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0354603)
- Google’s current audio supervised-tuning docs list “language learning: personalized feedback on pronunciation” as a use case. The training format is audio in, text out (one audio file per example). That teaches the model to emit a target string. It does not add a mechanism that checks filler counts or pitch. — [Gemini audio tuning docs, updated 2026-11-07](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/tuning/audio)

### Inferences
- (a) Replicating a rubric of filler rate, pace, and uptalk does not need a learned model. Those are counts and signal measures. A model trained to “predict” them from coach labels will be noisier than counting them, because the label already contains rater disagreement (pairwise Spearman only .470 even when raters were told to focus on delivery). — judgment from the Azaïs and SpeechRater findings above.
- (b) Predicting coach ratings (holistic 1–10 or dimension scores) is learnable. The ceiling is inter-rater agreement, not ground truth. On delivery, published systems often land near human–human correlation (SpeechRater 0.61 vs humans 0.63 at speaker level; meta-analysis delivery r = .784). On content, persuasion, and word choice, both humans and machines are weaker (persuasion reliability .41; content r = .574; grammar/vocabulary r = .499).
- (c) Generating coach-like feedback text is learnable as style imitation (audio-to-text supervised fine-tuning). The PLOS result is the warning: fluent coaching prose can be systematically too high and almost uncorrelated on phonemes (r = .271). A model can sound like a coach and still cite the wrong count.
- (d) Detecting articulation errors can be learned, but from phone- or word-level error labels (SpeechOcean762, GOP, mispronunciation detection), not from a holistic coach score. Expert agreement on *where* the error is was only kappa ≈ 0.49 in the ETS annotation study. Read-aloud CAPT with a reference transcript is a different problem from unscripted phone-mic monologues.

### Gaps
- No published study was found that trains a model on working public-speaking coaches’ free-text notes and shows those notes are more accurate than rubric scores.
- The ICMI 2015 r = 0.745 result does not report sample size in the abstract; the Chen et al. r = 0.765 result is 14 speakers. Neither is evidence that a production model at that correlation will hold for a consumer app.

## How much labeled speech do assessment and disfluency papers use, and how much do human coaches agree?

### Takeaway
Disfluency detectors are trained on on the order of 100 hours of already-annotated conversational speech and still drop sharply off-domain. Speaking-score models are often trained on 10^3 short responses, not on thousands of hours of coach-rated monologue. Human agreement is high for trained examiners on a fixed exam band, moderate for delivery, and poor for persuasion, word choice, and phone-error location. A model cannot be more “true” than that ceiling.

### Cited Findings
- Switchboard disfluency annotations used as a training resource in a 2025 benchmark comprise 189,428 segments and 121 hours. A BERT tagger trained on that text reached weighted F1 0.85 on Switchboard (filled pauses 1.00, repetitions 0.85, revisions 0.70, partial words 0.83) and weighted F1 0.76 zero-shot on FluencyBank Timestamped (adults who stutter). Weighted recall fell from 0.86 to 0.73. — [FluencyBank Timestamped, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379651/)
- The same paper’s audio model (Whisper encoder, frame every 20 ms, no transcript) reached only weighted F1 0.68 on Switchboard and 0.48 on FluencyBank. Weighted recall fell from 0.65 to 0.43 (described as a 34% drop). Repetition recall fell from 0.66 to 0.29. — [FluencyBank Timestamped, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379651/)
- Classic Switchboard disfluency experiments use the Penn Treebank Switchboard portion, split by directory (sections 2–3 train). One 2019 paper reports 169,494 training sentences in that split, not hours. — [Dong et al., AAAI 2019](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v33i01.33016351)
- The full Switchboard corpus is about 2,430 conversations, over 240 hours, about 3 million words, 500+ speakers, telephone bandwidth. Disfluency labels cover a subset, not the whole corpus. — [ISIP Switchboard overview](https://isip.piconepress.com/projects/switchboard/html/overview.html); [LDC annotation case study](https://aclanthology.org/L00-1213.pdf)
- Moving disfluency detection to a new domain without in-domain labels is brittle: a system trained on careful Switchboard transcripts scored F1 39.7 on Supreme Court oral-argument transcripts; in-domain careful annotation alone was F1 26.1; a transfer system reached F1 62.2. — [Zayats et al., NAACL 2015](https://aclanthology.org/N15-1161.pdf)
- SpeechRater-style scoring data in the sources above: about 1,200 responses to train, about 1,800 to evaluate; TOEFL Junior speaking human–machine r = .81 versus human–human r = .89 on five-item forms. — [ETS TOEFL Junior automated scoring report](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2015/jufc.html)
- Job-interview dialog scoring (non-native speakers): 123 conversations and 1,893 utterances (mean turn 6.3 s) for cross-validation; 365 utterances double-scored. Human–human correlation was fluency ρ = 0.54 (quadratic weighted kappa 0.48), pronunciation ρ = 0.61 (kappa 0.54), intonation/stress ρ = 0.57 (kappa 0.45). Gradient boosting and random forests matched or slightly exceeded those human correlations on some dimensions (fluency model correlation up to 0.58). — [Ramanarayanan et al., Interspeech 2017](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/ramanarayanan17b_interspeech.pdf)
- Trained IELTS-style examiners on a structured speaking test can agree tightly: SpeechAce reports exact grade match 21.8%, within 0.5 band 72.7%, within 1 band 98.2%, Cohen’s kappa 0.794, Pearson r = 0.883, RMSE 0.674, with a third rater when two differed by more than one point. — [SpeechAce speaking-test documentation](https://speaking-test-docs.speechace.com/speechace-speaking-test/speechace-speaking-test-documentation/test-development)
- Four examiners rescoring IELTS videos had Krippendorff’s α = 0.68 on overall criterion scores; dropping one rater raised α to 0.77. Agreement with official IELTS scores was α = 0.81, 0.78, 0.80, and 0.67. The sample of candidates in the published table is very small (two candidates shown in detail). — [Isaacs and Trofimovich, “The Rashomon effect”](https://doi.org/10.1080/19463014.2021.1923540)
- Oral-presentation analytic ratings: single-rater ICC .575 (95% CI .263–.778), average-of-raters ICC .730 (95% CI .417–.875). The paper cites Koo and Li (2016): 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good. — [Examining rater reliability for oral presentations](https://doi.org/10.70730/jqgy9980)
- Contractor raters on a constrained pronunciation / CEFR-band task, after filtering, reached quadratic weighted kappa .85, Spearman .84, and single-score ICC .85 on 421 double-rated items. The authors call that very good to excellent. — [Cai et al., Language Learning 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/lang.70000)
- Three pronunciation experts after a standardization workshop: ICC .882. — [PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0354603)
- Microsoft’s own interpretation table calls Pearson 0.1–0.3 low, 0.3–0.5 medium, and 0.5–1.0 high, and says its pronunciation assessment is >0.5 with human judges. That “high” bin starts at 0.5. — [Azure pronunciation assessment characteristics and limitations](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/responsible-ai/speech-service/pronunciation-assessment/characteristics-and-limitations-pronunciation-assessment)
- A vendor benchmark on SpeechOcean762 (2,500 non-native English recordings, Mandarin L1, five experts, read speech) reports Azure sentence-overall Pearson 0.7965, sentence fluency 0.7097, sentence accuracy 0.6728, word accuracy 0.5900. This is a competitor’s blog, not Microsoft’s paper, and “sentence overall” is not the same metric as Microsoft’s 0.6562 accuracy PCC. — [CHIVOX comparison blog](https://www.chivox.com/en/blog/benchmarking-chivox-vs-microsoft-azure-non-native-speech)

### Inferences
- “Good” in this literature is roughly: r or ICC around 0.6 is moderate and often close to what two raters manage on spontaneous delivery; r around 0.8 is what trained exam raters reach on a constrained scale, or what machines reach when many delivery features are aggregated. r = 0.8 is not “incredibly accurate” in the sense of matching a single coach’s note on a free monologue. Microsoft’s own marketing calls anything above 0.5 “high,” which is a low bar relative to the founder’s wording. — judgment, using the bands in Koo and Li as cited by the oral-presentation paper and Microsoft’s own table.
- If two coaches’ scores correlate at r = 0.63 (SpeechRater’s human baseline) or pairwise Spearman 0.47 (Azaïs), no model trained on those labels can be more true than that. Extra parameters fit one coach’s idiosyncrasies.
- Disfluency F1 of 0.85 is on manual transcripts of telephone conversation, for filled pauses and repetitions. It is not a model of “speaking quality,” and the audio-only version is much worse (weighted F1 0.68 in-domain, 0.48 on stuttered speech). Vojo already keeps fillers in the transcript; a custom audio disfluency model would be trying to replace a step the transcript already does more directly.
- Data scale that has actually produced published scoring models: hundreds to a few thousand short responses for a feature-based regressor; ~5,000 read utterances with five expert labels for pronunciation correlation around 0.65; ~121 hours for text disfluency tagging. None of these is “train on real coaches until the model is incredibly accurate.”

### Gaps
- No Krippendorff’s alpha was found for working public-speaking coaches scoring 60-second phone monologues on articulation, filler use, and uptalk together.
- The Rashomon IELTS alpha is on a tiny candidate set; it should not be quoted as the IELTS operational reliability.
- Exact hours of coach-labeled (as opposed to exam-rater-labeled) public-speaking audio in any public training set were not found. Azaïs used postgraduate students, not coaches, and told them to ignore content and pronunciation.

## What would it cost and require to have coaches label 60-second clips, and what privacy issues follow?

### Takeaway
US speech-language pathologists earn about $47 an hour in wages; annotation marketplaces advertise roughly $20–$50 an hour for non-expert tasks and up to about $40 an hour for voice *recording*. Expert coach labeling of scored clips will cost more than the wage, and the bill is dominated by human time, not GPUs. Storing speech for training is personal data; Illinois voiceprint law and 2026 lawsuits make speaker-embedding training a consent problem, especially for Illinois users.

### Cited Findings
- BLS May 2025: speech-language pathologists, 183,390 employed, mean hourly wage $47.20, median $47.05, 10th percentile $30.24, 90th percentile $64.50, median annual $97,870. — [BLS OEWS Table 1, May 2025](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ocwage.t01.htm); [BLS occupational profile](https://data.bls.gov/oesprofile/?areas=INDUSTRY%2CSTATE%2CMSA&major_group=290000&measure=01&occupation=291127&year=2025)
- Prolific, 8 January 2025: researchers set annotation rates starting at $20–$50 per hour, with higher pay for specialized knowledge. — [Prolific data-annotation guide](https://www.prolific.com/resources/everything-you-need-to-know-about-data-annotation-jobs)
- An Outlier (Scale AI) voice-recording role, removed July 2025, listed pay up to $40 USD per hour for recording short clips and reviewing others’ recordings. That is performance and review of recordings, not expert rubric scoring by a coach. — [Built In job posting](https://builtin.com/job/bring-your-voice-ai-training-hindi-india/6179125)
- Google’s supervised-tuning guidance says start with 100 examples and scale to thousands if needed, and that quality matters more than quantity. The tuning API text says “for best results, provide at least 100 to 500 examples.” Validation sets can contain up to 256 examples. — [Prepare supervised fine-tuning data](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/gemini-supervised-tuning-prepare); [Tuning API reference](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/model-reference/tuning)
- Illinois BIPA (740 ILCS 14/) defines biometric identifiers to include a voiceprint. Before collection, a private entity must give written notice of the purpose and retention term and obtain a written release, publish a retention and destruction schedule, and not sell or profit from the biometric data. Statutory damages cited in secondary legal commentary are $1,000 per negligent violation and $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation, plus fees. — [ABA litigation note on voiceprints and BIPA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/resources/newsletters/class-actions-derivative-suits/voiceprints-ai-bipa-new-trends-biometric-privacy-litigation/); [Code on Trial, May 2026 complaints](https://www.codeontrial.ai/p/voiceprints-under-bipa)
- Nine class actions filed in the Northern District of Illinois between 11 and 13 May 2026 allege that large labs extracted voiceprints from public recordings to train voice models without BIPA notice and consent. Commentary says the open legal question is whether a general voice model is a “voiceprint” or only a speaker-authentication template is. These are allegations, not holdings. — [Code on Trial](https://www.codeontrial.ai/p/voiceprints-under-bipa); [Capitol News Illinois](https://capitolnewsillinois.com/news/tech-giants-sued-over-stealing-voices-of-well-known-journalists-voice-actors-to-train-ai/)
- A practical privacy distinction used by one 2026 legal explainer: a recording used to train transcription is personal data; extracting a speaker embedding, building a voiceprint, or training a model to speak as a particular person is the point at which teams should treat the processing as biometric. — [ipto.ai voice-data consent note](https://www.ipto.ai/articles/voice-data-consent/)

### Inferences
- These cost figures are judgment, anchored to the wages and marketplace rates above, not to a Scale or Appen public price card (none found).
  - Numeric scores only (listen to 60 seconds, mark filler/pace/uptalk/overall): about 2–4 minutes of expert time per clip if the rubric is already written. At a loaded coach or SLP contractor rate of roughly $80–$150 per hour (above the $47 median wage, because contractors are not paid the BLS wage), that is about $3–$10 per clip per rater.
  - Scores plus a short coaching paragraph: about 5–10 minutes, about $8–$25 per clip per rater.
  - Double rating, which the reliability literature says you need, roughly doubles the labeling bill. Adjudication adds more.
- A first useful *evaluation and calibration* set, not a production model: on the order of 200–500 sixty-second clips, two coaches each, stratified by accent and phone model. At $10 per clip per rater, 400 clips × 2 raters ≈ $8,000 in rater time before recruiting, rubric design, and tooling. Judgment.
- A set large enough to *train* a scorer in the style of the published feature models (about 1,000–2,500 responses, multiple speakers, a held-out speaker split): low tens of thousands of dollars if scores only and raters are efficient; low hundreds of thousands if every clip gets written coaching from two professionals and you also need accent coverage. Judgment. GPU cost is small next to this (see next section).
- The binding constraint is speaker diversity and label agreement, not minutes. Chen et al. got r = 0.765 with 14 speakers; that number will not survive a new population. Azaïs needed 228 speakers to get ρ = 0.60 from acoustics alone.
- Consent: practice audio is personal data. Separate, specific consent is needed before using a user’s monologue to train or fine-tune a model, distinct from consent to grade that session. Illinois users are the sharp edge because voiceprints are named in BIPA and 2026 suits are testing whether training embeddings count. Do not assume a recording is safe to embed because it is not a login voiceprint; that question is in active litigation. Retention limits and a public destruction schedule matter if any speaker-specific template is stored.

### Gaps
- No 2025–2026 public rate card was found for Scale, Appen, or a US communication-coaching firm pricing “score this 60-second clip.”
- No source gave minutes per clip for expert public-speaking annotation. The 2–10 minute range is judgment.
- Whether a ranking model that never stores a per-user voice template is a BIPA voiceprint is unresolved in the sources. The May 2026 cases are complaints.

## Which 2026 model approaches are realistic, and what do they cost versus about $0.01 per session?

### Takeaway
Realistic options are a small scoring head on existing speech features or self-supervised embeddings, or supervised fine-tuning of a speech LLM to imitate feedback text. OpenAI’s current realtime audio models are not fine-tunable. Training GPU cost for a 7B LoRA run can be tens to a few hundred dollars; human labels cost more. Serving a custom model loses to the hybrid on a per-session basis at Vojo’s volume, because published speech-to-text for one minute is already a fraction of a cent.

### Cited Findings
- OpenAI’s GPT-Realtime-2 and GPT-Realtime-2.1 docs list the fine-tuning endpoint as not supported. They accept audio input and can emit audio, but that is inference, not a training API. — [GPT-Realtime-2](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime-2); [GPT-Realtime-2.1](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime-2.1)
- Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct has public supervised-fine-tuning recipes via ms-swift (LoRA and DeepSpeed ZeRO-2, custom JSONL with an audio path plus a text response) dating from August 2024, and third-party trainers (LlamaFactory, a community PR on the official repo). A LlamaFactory recipe recommends 4× H800 and reports about 1 hour 18 minutes for a LoRA run on a music-semantics set; a comment on the official repo says full fine-tune OOM’d on one 80 GB A800, while LoRA on 4× A800 used about 20–21 GB per GPU. — [ms-swift issue 1653](https://github.com/modelscope/ms-swift/issues/1653); [LlamaFactory Qwen2-Audio recipe](https://docs.llamafactory.com.cn/docs/documents/best-practice/speechEnhancement); [Qwen2-Audio PR 61](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen2-Audio/pull/61)
- Gemini supervised fine-tuning supports audio on Gemini 2.5 models (and the text-tuning model list also includes Gemini 3.5 Flash and Flash-Lite). Limits for Gemini 2.5 audio examples: 60 minutes, one file, 100 MB. The documented format is audio file plus instruction, with a text model response. A Google Cloud blog on Gemini 1.5 audio fine-tuning recommended 20 epochs, learning-rate multiplier 1, adapter size 4, and said ASR-like tasks may need more epochs. The page was still the operational guidance found; it is about 1.5-era hyperparameters, not a 2026 price. — [Audio tuning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/tuning/audio); [Supported SFT models](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/tuning/supervised-tuning/use); [Google Cloud blog on Gemini SFT](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/master-gemini-sft)
- Observed on-demand GPU rates in late 2026: one source lists A100 80 GB SXM4 at $1.59/hr and H100 SXM5 at $1.89/hr (verified from a provider API around 30 September 2026, page stating 31 October 2026). Another lists A100 SXM4 80 GB from $1.50/GPU-hour, checked 7 November 2026. A worked example there prices a short LoRA of a 20B model at well under $1 of GPU time and a larger multi-GPU job in the low tens of dollars, and warns that install time and failed runs dominate the training math. — [OpenGPU Radar, fine-tuning rates](https://opengpuradar.com/workloads/llm-fine-tuning); [QuantaCloud fine-tune cost guide](https://quantacloud.net/guides/estimate-fine-tuning-cost)
- Deepgram’s own pricing page: Nova-3 pre-recorded $0.0043 per minute monolingual pay-as-you-go ($0.0036 on Growth); Nova-3 streaming $0.0077 per minute monolingual. Billed per second. — [Deepgram pricing](https://deepgram.com/pricing)
- AssemblyAI’s pricing page: Universal-2 pre-recorded $0.15 per hour; Universal-3.5 Pro pre-recorded $0.21 per hour; Universal-Streaming $0.15 per hour; Universal-3.6 Pro realtime $0.45 per hour. Streaming bills WebSocket session time, including idle time. — [AssemblyAI pricing](https://www.assemblyai.com/pricing)
- Third-party roundups disagree with each other and partly with Deepgram’s page on streaming rates (one May 2026 aggregator swaps pre-recorded and streaming; an October 2026 comparison quotes Nova-3 streaming near $0.0048/min). The vendor pages above are the ones to use. — [speech.dev Nova-3 card](https://www.speech.dev/models/deepgram-nova-3/); [AssemblyAI comparison dated around 3 November 2026](https://www.assemblyai.com/blog/assemblyai-vs-deepgram)
- Adding non-native speech to an acoustic model helps pronunciation assessment. One study found the best alignment and assessment when the acoustic model was trained on manually labeled non-native speech, and that adding 10 hours of non-native data in a low-resource setup already improved alignment and scores versus native-only training. — [Shi et al., Interspeech 2023](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/shi23_interspeech.pdf)
- Microsoft’s pronunciation stack is not a small-team fine-tune: it uses large-scale speech-to-text, neural TTS to synthesize mispronunciations, then fine-tunes on expert labels, and still reports PCC 0.6562 against human parity 0.6787 on read speech. — [Microsoft pronunciation blog](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azure-ai-services-blog/speech-service-update-%E2%80%93-hierarchical-transformer-for-pronunciation-assessment/3740866)

### Inferences
- Three realistic families, in increasing cost and decreasing interpretability:
  1. Do not train a model for filler rate, words per minute, or uptalk. Keep the transcript-plus-Praat path. Use coach labels only to set thresholds and to check that the deterministic score ranks clips the way coaches do.
  2. A small regression or ranking head on features Vojo already computes, or on frozen wav2vec2/HuBERT/Whisper-encoder embeddings, to predict a coach’s dimension score. This is the SpeechRater / Ramanarayanan pattern. It can approach human–human correlation on delivery. It will not explain articulation errors it was not labeled for. Training compute is hours on one GPU, i.e. a few dollars to a few hundred dollars at the ~$1.50–$1.90 per GPU-hour rates above. Judgment on the dollar total: a few experimental runs plus mistakes, still under about $1,000 of GPU unless the team starts full-finetuning large audio LLMs.
  3. Supervised fine-tuning of Qwen2-Audio or Gemini on (audio, coach paragraph) pairs to imitate feedback style. Gemini’s docs explicitly allow this. It does not make counts accurate. OpenAI realtime audio fine-tuning is not offered on the current realtime model docs.
- Distilling coach *rankings* (clip A is better than clip B) is a reasonable label if coaches agree more on order than on a 1–10 number. No head-to-head paper was found that shows ranking labels beat rubric scores for public speaking. This is a design option, not a published win.
- Per 60-second session, published STT alone is about $0.0025 (AssemblyAI Universal-2 at $0.15/hour) to $0.0043 (Deepgram Nova-3 pre-recorded). The founder’s ~$0.01 for STT plus Haiku is in a plausible range for a short coaching call; a current Haiku token price was not re-fetched for this note, so the $0.01 figure should stay attributed to the product, not to a vendor page.
- Serving a custom 7B audio model: at $1.50–$1.59 per A100-hour, an always-on GPU is on the order of $1,100 per month before utilization (judgment: 730 × $1.55). A single 60-second forward pass, if it occupied the GPU for a few seconds, would be well under one cent of marginal compute, but a tiny app will pay for idle time, engineering, and eval, not for the marginal second. The hybrid’s marginal cost stays near one cent with no model to host.
- A from-scratch articulation model competitive with Azure pronunciation assessment is not realistic for this team. Azure’s published gap to human parity on read speech is already small (0.656 vs 0.679), and it sits on a production ASR plus synthetic-error pretraining. Spontaneous monologues without a reference text are harder than SpeechOcean762.

### Gaps
- No official 2026 price was found for Gemini audio supervised-tuning jobs or for hosting the tuned model per minute of audio.
- Hume was not checked in a primary doc in this pass; no Hume fine-tune price or accuracy number is included.
- Whether a newer Qwen audio model than Qwen2-Audio (Omni / later) has a cleaner official fine-tune path as of November 2026 was not verified. The recipes cited are for Qwen2-Audio-7B.
- No measurement was found of wall-clock GPU seconds to score one 60-second clip with Qwen2-Audio. The “few seconds, sub-cent marginal cost, idle GPU dominates” statement is judgment.

## What published accuracy is actually good, and where does a custom model lose to the hybrid?

### Takeaway
r ≈ 0.6 with humans is a normal published result and often matches rater agreement. r ≈ 0.8 is strong and usually comes from delivery features, trained exam raters, or read speech, not from open-ended coaching. The hybrid wins wherever the claim is a count. A custom model could only win on judgments the counts do not capture, and only after a coach-labeled test set shows that it beats the current grader by enough to matter.

### Cited Findings
- Speaker-level SpeechRater r = 0.61 vs human inter-rater r = 0.63; single-item exact agreement already matched humans in 2009 (57.8% vs 57.2%) while full-test correlation still trailed (0.57 vs 0.74). — [Handbook chapter](https://ebrary.net/76697/education/reliability_validity); [Zechner et al. 2009](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1016/j.specom.2009.04.009)
- Combined human plus SpeechRater PRMSE 0.83 beats either alone (0.76 and 0.75). The production design at ETS is hybrid, not model-replaces-human. — [ETS TOEFL insight PDF](https://www.in.ets.org/content/dam/ets-india/pdfs/toefl/toefl-ibt-insight-s1v3.pdf)
- Meta-analysis: delivery is the easy construct (r = .784) and grammar/vocabulary the hard one (r = .499). Content sits at r = .574. — [Language Testing 2025 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/02655322251387816)
- Azure pronunciation assessment’s own bar for “high” correlation is >0.5. On SpeechOcean762 their research system reached 0.656 versus human parity 0.678 on the accuracy comparison they published. — [Azure limitations](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/responsible-ai/speech-service/pronunciation-assessment/characteristics-and-limitations-pronunciation-assessment); [Microsoft blog table](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azure-ai-services-blog/speech-service-update-%E2%80%93-hierarchical-transformer-for-pronunciation-assessment/3740866)
- Human agreement itself spans a wide range: IELTS-examiner Pearson 0.883 on a structured test; public-speaking holistic reliability about .63; persuasion .41; dialog fluency ρ = 0.54; phone-error location kappa about 0.49 for experts. — [SpeechAce](https://speaking-test-docs.speechace.com/speechace-speaking-test/speechace-speaking-test-documentation/test-development); [ETS public-speaking report](https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12083); [Ramanarayanan et al. 2017](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/ramanarayanan17b_interspeech.pdf); [Loukina et al. 2015](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/loukina15b_interspeech.pdf)
- Listener comprehension tracked human TOEFL speaking scores far more closely than SpeechRater (0.97 and 0.81 vs 0.55 and 0.37). A delivery-heavy model can correlate with raters and still miss whether a listener understood the talk. — [Handbook chapter, citing Bridgeman, Powers, Stone, and Mollaun 2012](https://ebrary.net/76697/education/reliability_validity)

### Inferences
- For Vojo, “incredibly accurate” should be retired as a goal. The honest target is: match a panel of coaches about as well as those coaches match each other, on a pre-registered rubric, on phone audio from users who were not in training. That is r in the 0.6s for holistic delivery, higher only for traits that can be counted.
- The current hybrid loses least, and a custom model loses most, on: exact filler counts, speaking rate, and uptalk/pitch shape. Those are measured, not judged. A generative coach that “hears” eight “ums” when the transcript has three is a product bug even if the prose is warm.
- The hybrid is weaker where coaches judge things the features do not name: whether an example landed, whether the opening earned attention, whether stress made a word sound unsure. That is the Haiku content step. A custom model replaces it only if coach-labeled eval shows higher agreement than Haiku at acceptable cost. Nothing in the literature says a small team’s fine-tune will do that out of the box.
- ETS’s own operational result is the design argument: human plus machine beat either alone. Vojo’s analogue is deterministic delivery scores plus a language model for content, checked against coaches, not a single network that swallows the waveform and emits a grade.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that compares Claude Haiku coaching sentences to human coaches on the same clips.
- No paper was found on automatic uptalk detection accuracy on phone microphones.

## Which failure modes make “incredibly accurate” the wrong goal?

### Takeaway
Accent, device, and domain shift move scores for reasons that are not speaking skill. Models that imitate a coach can be systematically too generous and wrong about phonemes. Users can game a delivery score by sounding fluent while saying nothing. Phone audio is not the training domain of Switchboard, SpeechOcean762, or conference talks.

### Cited Findings
- Automatic pronunciation scorers inherit ASR training bias. A 2025 review states that early scorers struggled with L2 speech because acoustic models were built for L1 English, that L1, dialect, gender, and age change scores, and that recording device and audio quality are construct-irrelevant biases that high-stakes uses have to measure. — [Cai et al., Language Learning 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/lang.70000)
- Duolingo’s English Test group, describing a pronunciation model aimed at intelligibility rather than imitation of an L1 accent, reports lower scores for Windows devices (audio quality) and disparities by language family, and says they responded with balanced devices and language backgrounds, acoustic augmentation, and extra accented speech. — [Duolingo English Test research blog](https://blog.englishtest.duolingo.com/new-research-in-language-learning-a-pronunciation-scoring-model-built-around-intelligibility-not-imitation/)
- A 2026 methods paper argues that treating any deviation from a native canonical form as an error is the wrong norm, and that fine-tuning a mispronunciation detector on the target learner population (L2-ARCTIC) raised ROC–AUC from 0.72 to 0.85 versus a native-pretrained model. — [Applied Sciences, “Beyond Native Norms”](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/2/647)
- Acoustic models trained only on native speech are a worse starting point for pronunciation assessment than models trained with manually labeled non-native speech. — [Shi et al., Interspeech 2023](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/shi23_interspeech.pdf)
- Domain shift on disfluency: the same text model loses 15% relative weighted recall from Switchboard to stuttered speech, and the audio model loses more (weighted recall 0.65 to 0.43). Repetitions are the failure class. — [FluencyBank Timestamped](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379651/)
- Whisper, trained to produce “intended” speech, drops many disfluencies: on Switchboard it still transcribes 8.7% of filled pauses, 9.4% of repetitions, 43.1% of revisions, and 1.4% of partial words (higher on FluencyBank). A coaching product that needs fillers cannot use a cleanup ASR as the transcript. — [FluencyBank Timestamped](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379651/)
- Generative pronunciation scoring was higher than three experts on every sub-score and correlated r = .271 on phonemes. — [PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0354603)
- SpeechRater’s delivery-heavy score tracked lay listeners’ comprehension much worse than human raters did (0.55 and 0.37 vs 0.97 and 0.81). That is the empirical version of gaming: optimize the features the model has, miss the communicative point. — [Handbook chapter](https://ebrary.net/76697/education/reliability_validity)
- More than 400 utterances per dimension in the interview-dialog study were unscorable because of poor audio or unintelligible speech, out of 1,893 utterances. Phone and channel quality already removes a large share of data before any model runs. — [Ramanarayanan et al. 2017](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/ramanarayanan17b_interspeech.pdf)
- Azaïs raters were instructed to ignore pronunciation and content; the database is 26 women and 202 men, conference talks, not bathroom-mirror phone video. Reported accuracy is on that domain. — [Azaïs et al. 2015](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/azais15_interspeech.pdf)
- JALT 2026 abstract (conference 20–22 November 2026; results as summarized on the program page): Azure pronunciation scores on ~3,510 ICNALE samples tracked CEFR, but classroom data (n = 306) showed moderate-to-strong human correlations “while revealing weaker fluency alignment and substantial systematic human–machine calibration differences.” — [JALT 2026 abstract](https://events.jalt.org/event/73/contributions/2197/)

### Inferences
- Accent bias is not a side issue for an articulation model. If the training coaches share one accent norm, the model will punish everyone else and call that accuracy. The literature’s recommended target is intelligibility for a stated listener, not distance from a canonical native phone. Vojo’s users are not a single L1.
- Gaming: once filler rate and pace are shown to the user as the score, users can slow down, delete fillers, and flatten the talk. A learned holistic model trained on those same coaches will learn the same shortcuts unless content labels are in the loss. The hybrid at least keeps the content judgment in a separate step that can read the transcript.
- Feedback that sounds like a coach but cites wrong counts is the failure mode of target (c). Guardrail: any number in the coaching sentence (filler count, words per minute, number of uptalk phrases) should be copied from the deterministic scorer, not generated. Judgment, and it is the direct product implication of the PLOS phoneme result.
- Phone-mic domain shift: models in these papers were trained on telephone conversations, studio or conference recordings, or prompted read speech. A 60-second bathroom or commute recording will move pitch tracks and ASR filler rates. Local Praat on the user device at least sees the same mic the user used; a model trained on clean speech will not.

### Gaps
- No measurement was found of how much filler-count error increases from a headset to a phone mic for Deepgram or AssemblyAI specifically.
- No user study was found on people deliberately gaming an automated public-speaking score.

## What is worth building, and what is not?

### Takeaway
Build a coach-labeled set to calibrate thresholds and to evaluate the hybrid grader. Do not train a from-scratch articulation model in pursuit of incredible accuracy. A small learned ranker is optional later, and only if that eval set shows the hybrid is leaving agreement on the table.

### Cited Findings
- The best published operational pattern in this review is contributory scoring: SpeechRater plus a human beats either alone (PRMSE 0.83 vs 0.76 and 0.75). — [ETS TOEFL insight](https://www.in.ets.org/content/dam/ets-india/pdfs/toefl/toefl-ibt-insight-s1v3.pdf)
- Machine–human correlations have improved into the high 0.6s overall and high 0.7s for delivery, and they still sit at human-agreement level rather than above it. — [Language Testing meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/02655322251387816); [SpeechRater r = 0.61 vs human r = 0.63](https://ebrary.net/76697/education/reliability_validity)
- Google’s own tuning guidance says 100–500 good examples are the right start for supervised fine-tuning, and quality beats quantity. That is enough to fit a style, not enough to establish a new accuracy ceiling. — [Tuning API](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/model-reference/tuning)
- Expert phone-error localization agreement is only kappa ≈ 0.49, and generative phoneme scoring correlates r = .271 with experts. Articulation “accuracy” is not a solved label. — [Loukina et al. 2015](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/loukina15b_interspeech.pdf); [PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0354603)
- Marginal STT cost for one minute is about $0.0025–$0.0043 at list price. — [AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/pricing); [Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/pricing)

### Inferences
- Worth building now (judgment, sized from the studies above):
  - A written rubric that separates countable delivery (fillers, pace, uptalk) from judged content.
  - Two or three coaches, double-scoring 200–500 real Vojo 60-second clips, plus a short note. Budget on the order of $5,000–$20,000 in rater time if rates are $80–$150 per hour and clips take a few minutes; written coaching on every clip costs more. This is a judgment range, not a quote.
  - Use the set first as an eval: agreement among coaches (ICC or Krippendorff), agreement of the current deterministic scores with coaches, and a blind preference between Haiku’s paragraph and a coach’s paragraph. If coaches disagree with each other more than they disagree with the hybrid, there is no accuracy left to buy with a model.
  - Use the same labels to move thresholds (how many fillers is “a lot” for this product), not to train a network.
- Optional later, only if the eval says delivery scores are mis-ranked: a small model on frozen embeddings or existing features to predict the coach average. Expect, if the project goes well, correlation in the 0.6s, similar to SpeechRater, not a new state of the art. Timeline judgment: a few months for a careful tiny team, most of it labeling and error analysis, not training.
- Not worth it: a from-scratch articulation model; fine-tuning a speech LLM as the source of filler counts; replacing Deepgram/AssemblyAI with a self-trained verbatim ASR; any plan whose success metric is “incredibly accurate.” OpenAI will not fine-tune the current realtime audio models. Gemini or Qwen2-Audio can imitate coach text, and that imitation is a style feature, not an accuracy feature.
- Timeline judgment for a production custom scorer that is merely “as good as published delivery scoring” (r near the human ceiling on held-out phone audio, multiple accents): 6–18 months, because the work is rubric design, recruiting coaches, consent, double labeling, and speaker-independent eval. The GPU bill is the small part. A team that skips the eval set and fine-tunes on a few hundred clips will get a model that sounds like a coach and fails the failure modes above.

### Gaps
- No source states a dollar budget or month count for a three-person app team to reach a given correlation. The ranges in this section are judgment.
- Coach wage rates for “communication coach” as distinct from BLS speech-language pathologists were not found.
